Two-Unit Conversions: System Strategies

Installing independent HVAC systems for each unit in a subdivided farmhouse requires isolating equipment, ductwork, and controls so tenants aren't sharing thermostats or paying for each other's energy use. The cleanest approach is to dedicate one system per unit, sized to that unit's square footage and heating/cooling load, with equipment located either in the unit's basement/attic footprint or in a shared mechanical room with separate utility meters.

Ductless mini-splits are the most common solution in Lebanon farmhouse subdivisions because they require minimal building penetration and eliminate the need for extensive ductwork in thick-walled construction. Each unit gets its own outdoor condenser and one or more indoor heads.

Refrigerant lines and condensate drains run through small core-drilled holes in the exterior walls—typically 3 inches in diameter—and the lines can be surface-mounted along baseboards or concealed in shallow chases.

Your HVAC Options for Farmhouse Subdivision

Ductless Mini-Splits

One outdoor condenser per unit, indoor wall-mounted heads. Minimal wall penetration (3-inch core holes). No ductwork needed. Typical cost: $5,500–$9,000 per unit. Quietest option (25–35 dB). Independent metering and control.

Basement Furnace + Ducts

Horizontal furnace in basement, ductwork through joist spaces. Requires fire-rated separation and combustion air louvers. Height constraints in 6-foot basements. Cost: $6,000–$11,000 per unit. Provides fresh-air ventilation integration.

Attic Air Handler (2nd Floor)

Condensing unit outside, air handler mounted in attic. Ducts drop through wall chases to each room. Avoids basement clutter. Cost: $5,500–$10,000 per unit. Requires attic access and insulation work.

Hybrid Heat Pump + Propane

Heat pump (25°F lockout), propane furnace backup for extreme cold. Cuts heating cost 30–40%. Requires dual-fuel coordination and propane tank. Cost: $8,000–$13,000 per unit. Best for high-heating-load farmhouses.

Drilling Through Limestone Without Damage

Penetrating the exterior walls of Lebanon's stone farmhouses for refrigerant lines, exhaust flues, or fresh air intakes requires the right equipment and technique. A standard hole saw or masonry bit will bind or break in limestone—the stone is dense enough to dull carbide quickly. Core drills with diamond-grit bits are the professional standard.

A 3-inch core bit mounted on a drill rig can penetrate 24 inches of limestone in 10 to 15 minutes with water cooling to control dust and prevent thermal cracking. Before drilling, the wall composition should be confirmed with a test hole. Some farmhouses have double-wythe walls with rubble-fill cavities between inner and outer stone layers. Loose rubble left in the hole after drilling can shift over time and pinch refrigerant lines.

After line installation, seal the hole with expanding foam or hydraulic cement, then point the exterior with matching lime mortar so the penetration is weathertight.

Code Compliance Checklist for Multi-Unit Conversions

  • One-hour fire-rated separation between all dwelling units (5/8-inch Type X drywall, fire-rated caulk at all penetrations)
  • Separate HVAC systems and utility metering for each unit—no shared ductwork or thermostats allowed
  • Fresh air ventilation for each unit (50–100 CFM depending on size and bedroom count via ERV or dedicated outdoor air intakes)
  • Combustion air for fuel-burning appliances—dedicated outdoor air intakes or sufficient room volume to supply furnace/boiler
  • Egress windows in any bedroom in basement or attic units (5.7 sq. ft. minimum openable area, 24-inch minimum height and width)
  • Fire dampers at duct penetrations through floor/ceiling assemblies if diameter exceeds code threshold
  • Separate electrical services and disconnect switches for HVAC equipment serving each unit

Basement Height Constraints and Solutions

Limestone farmhouse basements in Lebanon County are typically 6 to 6.5 feet tall—low by modern standards—with stone walls, dirt or flagstone floors, and minimal headroom. When installing HVAC equipment in a 6-foot basement, every inch counts. A standard upright furnace is 58–60 inches tall, plus filter cabinet adds 4 inches, and you need 24 inches of clearance above for flue routing and service. That's 86–90 inches total—7 to 7.5 feet—which doesn't fit.

Horizontal furnaces solve the height problem by orienting airflow sideways, so the unit is only 24–30 inches tall and can be mounted on a platform or suspended from ceiling joists. Ductwork routing is equally constrained—trunk lines running perpendicular to joists have to drop below joist bottoms, eating up 10–12 inches of ceiling height. Run ducts parallel to joists wherever possible to preserve basement usability.

In subdivisions where basement space is tight and multiple furnaces would crowd each other, ductless mini-splits eliminate the problem entirely—the outdoor condenser sits outside, indoor heads mount in living spaces, and the basement stays clear.

Common Comfort Issues in Subdivided Farmhouses

Asymmetric heating and cooling loads occur because units occupy different building zones. First floors benefit from basement residual heat and stone thermal mass; second floors bake under low-slope roofs in summer and lose heat through attics in winter. If systems are sized identically, the second-floor tenant will be uncomfortable year-round.

  • Thermostat placement matters—exterior stone walls read colder than actual room temperature in winter; interior locations with remote sensors solve the problem without relocating the base unit.
  • Sound transmission between units is common because floor assemblies and walls were never designed for acoustic separation; furnace blower noise in the first floor can be audible in the second floor if ductwork is poorly isolated.
  • Outdoor condenser noise (50–60 dB at 10 feet) requires thoughtful placement away from bedroom windows and neighboring property lines to avoid tenant complaints.
  • Attic and basement insulation gaps drive high utility bills—stone walls have thermal mass but no insulation; uninsulated attics and basements double heating/cooling costs compared to modern buildings.
  • Moisture trapped between stone walls and interior insulation causes weeping and odor in spring; verify interior wall assembly before adding vapor barriers or closed-cell spray foam.

The Landlord's Perspective: Why Code Compliance Matters

Cutting corners on mechanical systems in a farmhouse subdivision creates liability. Shared furnaces violate landlord-tenant law. Unpermitted conversions fail inspections when properties change hands. Undersized equipment leads to tenant turnover, complaints, and repair costs that dwarf initial savings. Invest in properly sized, independently controlled systems from the start. Ductless mini-splits are cost-effective and code-compliant. Basement furnaces require careful ductwork planning and fire-separation details.

Either way, the cost of doing it right ($10K–$18K for two units) is recovered within 3–5 years through reduced tenant turnover and lower liability exposure. Lebanon's farmhouses deserve better stewardship, and the tenants living in them deserve systems that actually work.

Farmhouse Subdivision and HVAC: What You Need to Know

Can I legally subdivide a farmhouse without upgrading the HVAC system?

No. Pennsylvania code requires separate HVAC systems and utility metering for each dwelling unit. Shared systems violate landlord-tenant law and create liability. If you're subdividing a farmhouse, plan for independent HVAC from the start—ductless mini-splits are usually the most cost-effective solution.

How much does it cost to install separate HVAC systems in a two-unit farmhouse conversion?

Expect $10,000 to $18,000 total for two independent ductless mini-split systems (one per unit), including outdoor condensers, indoor heads, electrical work, and core drilling for line sets. Forced-air systems with basement furnaces and ductwork run $12,000 to $22,000 depending on ductwork complexity and fire-separation requirements.

Why are the heating costs so high in these old stone houses?

Limestone walls have thermal mass but minimal insulation—they moderate temperature swings but don't prevent heat loss. Windows are often single-pane and leaky. Attics and basements are uninsulated. Heating a subdivided farmhouse costs 30–50% more than a comparably sized modern apartment because the building envelope is inefficient. Tenants should expect higher utility bills.

What's a heat pump lockout, and why does it matter in Lebanon winters?

Heat pumps lose efficiency below 25–30°F. Below that temperature, they switch to backup heat (electric resistance or propane). In Lebanon County's sub-zero winters, a heat pump alone will run expensive resistance heat for weeks. A hybrid system with propane backup cuts heating cost 30–40% by letting the furnace take over during extreme cold, so the heat pump doesn't have to run inefficiently.

Can I run ductwork through the walls instead of around them?

No. Limestone farmhouse walls are solid masonry without cavities. Running ducts requires either surface-mounting them (visible but affordable) or furred-out chases (frames built against the wall to hide ducts, but this consumes floor space). In subdivided units where square footage is already tight, surface-mounted ducts on interior walls are often the only practical option.

Farmhouse Heating Red Flags

  • Oil tank over 20 years old — leak liability exceeds remaining fuel savings; removal plus conversion to propane often cheaper than tank replacement.
  • Stone walls weeping in spring — moisture trapped between wall and any interior insulation; requires wall diagnostics before sealing.
  • Furnace struggles below 20°F — single-fuel system undersized for extreme cold; hybrid heat pump + propane conversion recommended.
  • Addition stays cold while original house overheats — two structures on one undersized system; separate equipment needed.
  • Propane bills exceeding $3,000/winter — dual-fuel conversion cuts 40% and pays for itself in 3–4 years.
