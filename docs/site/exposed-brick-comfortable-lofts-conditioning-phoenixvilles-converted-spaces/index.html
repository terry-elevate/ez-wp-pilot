The rowhouse retrofit process

  • Step 1: Load calculation for geometry — Standard HVAC sizing fails in rowhouses. Party walls are thermally neutral—your neighbor's heat is your heat. You lose through front, rear, and roof only. A proper Manual J accounts for shared-wall geometry and narrows your equipment size by 15–20 percent versus what a calculator would suggest for a detached home of the same square footage.
  • Step 2: Envelope assessment and air-sealing — Before any equipment goes in, we find where air leaks. Most rowhouse heat loss is infiltration, not conduction. Caulking rim joists, sealing the attic-stairwell gap, and weatherstripping windows can cut load by 20 percent. This step happens first because it changes what equipment size you actually need and what you'll pay to run it.
  • Step 3: System type selection — You choose from three paths: mini-splits (ductless, non-invasive), high-velocity hidden ducts (unobtrusive central air), or radiant plus supplemental cooling (comfort-first, space-efficient). Each trades off visibility, noise, comfort feel, and installation cost. We walk through the trade-offs for your specific floor plan and priorities.
  • Step 4: Equipment sizing and zone design — Rowhouses stack vertically, so zones matter more than in a flat ranch. We design separate thermostats for first floor, second floor, and third floor. Mini-split heads or duct branches are placed to fight the stack effect and avoid dead zones in narrow rooms. Equipment is sized for the corrected load, not oversized like suburban systems often are.
  • Step 5: Installation with minimal disruption — Ductless lines run up exterior rear walls in a conduit chase. High-velocity ducts snake through existing cavities. Radiant floor systems are tied to new or existing boilers. We preserve plaster, protect original windows, and route lines to be as invisible as possible while keeping the system functional and serviceable for decades.

Why rowhouse geometry breaks standard HVAC

Standard residential HVAC assumes you have attic ductwork space, a basement for equipment, and walls deep enough for branch runs. Phoenixville rowhouses offer none of these. Rubble-stone foundations lack full basements. Attics—if they exist—are often finished bedrooms. Walls are true 2×4 with lath and plaster: 3.5 inches of cavity, zero margin for error. Exterior brick has no sheathing cavity to work with. This geometry forces creative solutions.

Some homeowners run exposed basement ductwork and accept head-clearance loss. Others build central chases through closets, sacrificing storage for a main trunk. A growing number skip ducts entirely and use mini-splits in each room, treating HVAC like an add-on appliance. Each approach has comfort trade-offs: exposed ducts sweat in summer, central chases create pressure imbalances, mini-splits require careful head placement to avoid dead zones. Heat-loss calculations also differ.

Because party walls are thermally neutral, most loss happens through front, rear, and roof. The facades are small—maybe 16 feet wide—so overall loss looks manageable on paper. In reality, poor attic insulation and single-pane windows create fierce drafts. The third floor might be 15 degrees colder than the living room two floors below. Right-sizing means understanding zone-by-zone air distribution, not just total BTU.

Three system types for rowhouses

Mini-splits

Ductless. Wall-mounted heads in each room. Outdoor condenser on rear patio. No invasive ductwork. Quieter than forced air. Works in narrow floor plans. Trade-off: visible indoor units, potential dead zones in 12×20 bedrooms.

High-velocity ducts

Central air via 2-inch flexible ducts in walls. Small round outlets blend into ceiling. Compact air handler in closet or nook. Retrofit-friendly. No plaster demolition needed. Trade-off: audible whoosh sound, more outlets needed, louder than conventional systems.

Radiant heat plus cooling

Restore boiler and radiators or upgrade to baseboard hot water. Keep comfort of radiant heating. Add mini-split or window AC for summer. Hybrid approach. Preserves character. No ductwork needed. Trade-off: separate systems, cooling still requires equipment, radiators occupy wall space.

Mini-split performance in narrow rooms

Ductless mini-splits have become the default for Phoenixville rowhouse retrofits. No ductwork needed. Installation is non-invasive. You can heat and cool unconditioned spaces without a gut job. Typical setup: one outdoor condenser on rear patio, three to five indoor heads across floors. Living room and kitchen share one head, each bedroom gets a wall unit, sometimes a ceiling cassette in the hallway.

Refrigerant lines run up the exterior rear wall inside a PVC chase. Only interior penetrations are small holes for line sets and mounting brackets. The challenge is rooms 12 feet wide and 20 feet deep. Most mini-split heads throw air horizontally, optimized for square rooms or open plans.

In a narrow bedroom, air blasts the far wall and bounces back, creating a dead zone, or dumps cold air in the corner farthest from the door, leaving the hallway side warm. Ceiling cassettes distribute in four directions but require drop ceilings or bulkheads that cost height many owners refuse to lose.

Wall units work better on the short wall near the door, blowing lengthwise, but that's where the closet or window often is. The stairwell effect is overlooked. Because these homes are vertical tubes, warm air rises aggressively in winter, leaving the first floor cold unless the living room mini-split runs on high.

In summer, cool air sinks and pools on the first floor while the third-floor bedroom stays warm despite its own unit running. Most successful installations treat each floor as a separate zone with its own thermostat strategy, rather than expecting the system to automatically balance three stories of air.

What to expect during conversion

  • Week 1: Site survey and load calculation — We measure every room, photograph existing systems, test air leakage, and inspect the attic and basement. Manual J software gets updated with rowhouse geometry factors. You get a written heating and cooling load for each floor zone.
  • Week 2: System design and quotes — Based on your load and chosen system type, we design ductwork layout (if applicable), mini-split head placement, or radiant upgrades. You receive three quotes: baseline, mid-range with better efficiency, and premium. Timelines and costs are itemized.
  • Week 3–4: Permitting and ordering — We handle gas/electric permitting with the Borough. Equipment is ordered. For mini-splits, we schedule the outdoor unit install and line-set run first, so the back wall is ready before indoor heads arrive.
  • Week 5–6: Installation phase — Outdoor condenser is installed and tested. Refrigerant lines are evacuated and charged. Indoor heads are mounted and wired. Thermostats are programmed. If radiant work is involved, boiler is swapped and baseboard hot-water lines are flushed. High-velocity ductwork takes longer—expect 2–3 weeks for flex ducts, sealing, and outlet placement.
  • Week 7: Testing, balancing, and handoff — We test heating and cooling across all zones. Thermostats are calibrated. You get a one-year service plan, filter schedules, and emergency contact info. We return in 30 days to verify no new issues have cropped up during the first heating/cooling cycle.

Insulation and air-sealing come first

No HVAC system performs well if the envelope hasn't been addressed. These homes were built with no wall insulation, maybe a few inches of sawdust in the attic, and original wavy glass windows with no weatherstripping. Front and rear brick walls have no cavity to insulate. Attics are often finished bedrooms, so adding insulation means losing ceiling height or doing expensive exterior foam retrofit. But air-sealing is achievable and high-impact.

The biggest leaks are at rim joists, the stairwell-attic junction, and window sashes. A day with a caulk gun and expanding foam cuts heating load 20 percent, letting you install a smaller, cheaper HVAC system. Weatherstripping windows is critical but must be done carefully—old frames often don't close evenly. Some owners install interior storm windows, adding an air gap without modifying the historic exterior.

Attic insulation is the final piece: if the attic is unfinished, blow in cellulose or fiberglass to R-40 for a few thousand dollars. That investment pays back faster than any equipment upgrade. If the attic is a finished bedroom, your options are limited—accept lower insulation, furr out the ceiling, or do an exterior job when you re-roof.

Right-sizing HVAC without addressing the envelope is like trying to fill a bucket with a hole in the bottom.

Radiant heat in Phoenixville rowhouses

Some Phoenixville rowhouses still have original steam or hot-water radiators, and you have a decision: restore it, replace it, or supplement with modern cooling. A functioning radiator system is a rare asset. Radiant heat is comfortable, silent, and forgiving of poor insulation because it warms surfaces and people, not air.

If radiators and piping are intact and the boiler can be serviced or replaced with a high-efficiency unit, keeping the radiant system is often the most cost-effective and comfortable path to reliable heat. You'll still need separate cooling—mini-splits, window units, or high-velocity AC—but radiant heat provides a quality of comfort hard to replicate with forced air.

The problem arises when you want to modernize and radiators are in the way. Cast-iron columns take up 18 inches of wall space in already narrow rooms. They're fixed in place, limiting furniture and built-in options. Removing them means converting to baseboard hot water (still occupies wall space) or abandoning radiant heat entirely. Some owners install radiant floor systems during gut renovations, embedding PEX tubing in new subfloors.

This gives radiant comfort with no visible equipment but is expensive, raises floor height by an inch, and still doesn't solve cooling. A hybrid approach is common: keep radiators on floors one and two where you spend most time and where aesthetics work, add mini-splits for cooling and supplemental heat on floor three.

Boiler runs radiators in winter, mini-splits handle cooling in summer and boost heat on coldest nights when radiators can't quite keep up. It's not elegant system design, but in a rowhouse where every decision is a compromise, it's often the most livable one.

High-velocity duct systems

If you want central air without bulkheads, high-velocity systems like Unico or SpacePak offer a middle path. These use flexible 2-inch ducts that snake through walls and floors with minimal demolition, and the air outlets are small round grilles that blend into ceilings or high walls.

  • Retrofit into existing walls and ceilings without tearing out plaster, ideal for homes where preserving original finishes is a priority.
  • Flexible ducts bend around joists and studs that would block rigid sheet-metal runs.
  • Compact air handler fits in closets or small basement nooks. Outdoor condenser matches standard AC unit size, feasible even without attic or ample mechanical space.
  • Audibly louder than conventional forced air—a 'whooshing' sound near outlets. In quiet rowhouses with shared walls, can be intrusive at night.
  • Small outlets require more registers to cover the same area. A 12×20 bedroom might need two or three outlets instead of one.
  • High-velocity air mixes faster and reduces stratification, but some find the sensation uncomfortable compared to gentle standard register waft. Personal preference matters.

Phoenixville's microclimates and cooling needs

Phoenixville sits in a river valley where the Schuylkill and French Creek converge, creating cooling patterns that vary block by block. Homes on hill streets—Main, Church, Starr—catch more breeze in summer and have better natural ventilation than rowhouses in the flats near the old mill site. Those lower blocks, especially around Vanderslice and Franklin, trap heat on still summer evenings and have less airflow to flush it out.

If you're buying or renovating there, plan for higher cooling loads and consider a slightly larger AC system than Manual J suggests. Conversely, homes on ridgeline streets are more exposed to winter wind, increasing infiltration and making air-sealing critical. Neighborhoods near French Creek have higher humidity because of water proximity, affecting cooling efficiency and mold risk in basements.

A whole-house dehumidifier is smart if your home is within a few blocks of the creek, especially if you're finishing a basement or have chronic mustiness.

Common HVAC questions for Phoenixville rowhouses

Can I install central air in a rowhouse without losing ceiling height?

High-velocity systems like Unico use 2-inch flexible ducts that run through existing walls and floor cavities with minimal soffits or bulkheads. They're not invisible, but far less invasive than conventional 8-inch ducts. Expect small round ceiling outlets instead of rectangular registers, and slightly higher noise. If you're doing a gut renovation, you can plan chase spaces and use standard ducts, but for a cosmetic rehab keeping the plaster, high-velocity is usually the only viable central-air option.

Is it worth keeping my old radiators if the boiler still works?

If radiators and piping are intact and the boiler can be serviced or replaced with a modern high-efficiency unit, keeping the radiant heat system is often the most cost-effective and comfortable option for heating. You'll need a separate cooling solution—mini-splits, window units, or high-velocity AC—but radiant heat provides comfort that's hard to replicate with forced air.

The main reason to remove radiators is if you need the wall space for furniture or built-ins, or if piping is corroded and leaking. Even then, consider baseboard hot water as a replacement before abandoning radiant entirely.

Why is my third-floor bedroom always hotter or colder than the rest of the house?

Rowhouses stack vertically, so hot air rises aggressively up the stairwell in winter while cool air sinks in summer. If your attic or top-floor ceiling is poorly insulated, that effect is even worse.

The solution is threefold: add as much attic insulation as you can, install a dedicated mini-split or register so the top floor has its own zone control, and consider a small fan at the top of the stairwell to push air back down in winter or draw it up in summer.

Some homeowners also close the bedroom door and treat it as a sealed zone rather than expecting equilibration with the rest of the house.

What does a rowhouse load calculation actually cost?

A Manual J assessment for a rowhouse typically runs $300–500. It includes a site visit, measurements of every room, attic and basement inspection, air-leakage testing, and a written report with heating and cooling loads by zone. That cost is usually credited toward installation if you move forward with us, so it's an investment in getting the system right, not an expense that evaporates. Without it, you're guessing at equipment size and efficiency, which costs far more in the long run.

Can I do mini-splits in a rowhouse without the outdoor unit being visible?

The outdoor condenser is about the size of a standard AC unit and needs to be on the rear patio or side alley, where it's visible to neighbors and pedestrians. Refrigerant lines run up the exterior wall inside a painted PVC conduit that's designed to blend with brick mortar. Some homeowners screen the condenser with a lattice structure or plantings, but there's no way to make it truly invisible.

If visibility is a concern, it's a trade-off you're making by choosing mini-splits over radiant or high-velocity ductwork.
