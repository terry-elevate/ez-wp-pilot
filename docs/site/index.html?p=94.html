Why ducts lose the argument

Retrofitting central air into a Pittsburgh rowhouse means finding vertical paths for supply and return trunks through a house that has none. The party walls on both sides are structural masonry — off limits. The interior walls are plaster over wood lath, usually load-bearing along the stair.

Three stacked floors over a walk-out basement behave like a chimney: heated air migrates up and out through the top level while cold air is drawn in below. A single air handler in the basement, pushing through improvised ductwork, fights that vertical gradient from the worst possible position. Ductless sidesteps all of it.

Refrigerant lines are under an inch in diameter, they route through a single small penetration per head, and each head conditions the floor it sits on instead of relying on air pushed up from below.

Line-set routing on brick facades

The refrigerant lines, condensate drain, and control wire between each indoor head and the outdoor unit have to travel down the outside of the building. On brick rowhouses that run is the most visible decision in the whole project. The default should be the rear elevation — the yard-facing wall almost nobody sees — with lines dropping in a straight vertical run inside painted line-hide channel.

Penetrations deserve equal care: drill through mortar joints, not brick faces, so the hole remains repairable if equipment is ever removed. Condensate has its own gravity problem on a slope. A head on the downhill side may drain to daylight; the uphill side sits below grade and needs a small condensate pump. Neither is a defect — both need to be in the plan before install week.

Six numbers to settle before drilling brick

Rowhouse ductless jobs go wrong in planning, not installation. These six measurements must appear in writing before any quote is final — if a proposal arrives without them, it was written for a generic house, not yours.

The six-point design audit

  • Per-floor heat loss and gain, calculated from wall construction and window count
  • Wall composition at each penetration — multi-wythe brick, veneer, or block — dictates drilling method
  • Total line-set length per zone, checked against manufacturer limits that tall houses approach faster
  • Electrical panel capacity and breaker availability for dedicated 240V circuit
  • Condensate path for every head — gravity drain or pump — marked before pricing
  • Outdoor unit placement sketched on actual elevation with snow depth and party-wall clearance

Condenser mounting options for hillside lots

Rear gravel pad

Leveled composite base on graded yard. Best option when slope allows. Straight line-set run, minimal vibration transmission.

Wall-mounted bracket

Bolted into masonry with vibration isolators. Common in this city. Must be above walkways and snow drifts. Never hangs over neighbor's path.

Terraced deck mount

Porch roof or addition roof with engineered stand. Requires structural review. Elevates unit above snow and roof drain lines.

Hillside neighborhoods: site-specific routing

Allegheny County's river valleys built vertically because terrain gave them no choice. The pattern repeats from South Side Slopes to Polish Hill to Spring Hill to streets above the West End. Lawrenceville and Bloomfield add a flatter variant — long, deep rowhouses where the vertical problem is milder but the no-ductwork constraint is identical.

Historic district designations may require approval for equipment visible from the street, which is one more argument for rear-elevation line-set routing. The rivers themselves contribute muggy summer air: a ductless head running long, low-speed cooling cycles wrings moisture far better than window units slamming on and off — which is the upgrade most third-floor bedrooms in this city are actually waiting for.

Duquesne Light serves the entire grid and offers demand-response credits for homes with smart thermostats. Peoples Gas delivers through these narrow hillside streets and covers high-efficiency furnace installs. On South Side Slopes between 18th and 21st, condenser placement often requires wall brackets — yards slope too steeply for ground pads.

In Troy Hill along Lowrie Street, each brick rowhouse has a unique rear elevation — cookie-cutter plans fail because no two pathways are alike.

Hillside rowhouse comfort diagnosis

  • Top floor 10+ degrees warmer than basement level — vertical zoning needed
  • Condensers on hillside grade shifting position — mounting pad or bracket required
  • Line-set routing visible on front facade — aesthetic routing through rear preferred
  • Party-wall side comfortable, exterior side cold — directional heat loss confirms insulation gaps
  • Stairwell acts as chimney in winter — stack effect pulling heated air upward and out

Rowhouse ductless questions we hear in Pittsburgh

One multi-zone condenser or separate outdoor units per floor?

Usually one multi-zone outdoor unit, because hillside lots rarely have room for two or three. Separate units win when floors have wildly different schedules — a rented ground floor, for instance — or when a single unit's minimum output exceeds the smallest head's needs. The load math decides it, not a default.

Will wall penetrations hurt the brick or the house's value?

A correctly sleeved three-inch hole through a mortar joint is a repairable, reversible modification — repointing restores the joint if equipment is removed. What damages masonry is careless drilling through brick faces and unsealed penetrations. The method is the whole difference.

Can ductless really carry a Pittsburgh January without the boiler?

Cold-climate models hold strong output at five degrees and keep operating well below zero, covering nearly all local winter hours. Many owners keep an existing boiler as backup for deepest cold and resale flexibility. Houses with aging boilers often retire them entirely. Both designs are legitimate when sized honestly.

How much does a three-zone rowhouse system cost installed?

Multi-zone systems range from $8,500 to $15,000 installed, depending on wall composition, line-set routing complexity, and condenser mounting method. Rear-elevation bracket mounts cost less than wall-drilled penetrations on masonry that requires specialized bits. Request an actual site walkthrough for accurate pricing.
