Why visual inspection fails in a six-month heating season

Altoona heats longer and harder than most of Pennsylvania. The city sits roughly twelve hundred feet up, wedged between Brush Mountain and the Allegheny Front, and the valley holds its cold — furnaces here routinely fire from early October into April. A machine asked to run six months out of twelve deserves more than a vacuuming and a thumbs-up before the season opens.

Yet most of what gets sold as a tune-up is exactly that: pull the doors, brush the burners, eyeball the flame, gone in twenty minutes. Combustion problems do not announce themselves to eyeballs.

They announce themselves as numbers — oxygen percentage, carbon monoxide concentration, stack temperature, draft pressure — and a technician who never puts an analyzer probe into your flue has not actually tested whether your furnace is burning safely or wastefully.

Four flue readings and what they reveal

Natural gas burns blue, and a tidy blue flame looks reassuring. But flame color is a crude signal. Proper combustion is a ratio problem — a specific gas input meeting a specific volume of air — and either side of that ratio can drift without changing how the fire looks. Dust chokes the burner intakes, gas pressure creeps as a regulator ages, flue restriction slows the exhaust.

The flame stays blue long after the chemistry has gone off. The byproduct that matters most is carbon monoxide, produced whenever the fire runs short of oxygen. It is invisible and odorless, and at the concentrations where a technician should intervene, it changes nothing you can see or smell at the burner. The only honest way to know is to sample the flue and read the number.

  • Oxygen: 6–9% healthy; lower runs rich (CO risk), higher wastes heat up the chimney
  • Carbon monoxide: under 100 ppm stable; rising CO when blower starts signals heat exchanger crack
  • Stack temperature: 350–450°F for standard furnace; ~110°F for condensing — tells you whether the exchanger is working
  • Draft pressure: measured in hundredths of an inch water column — confirms chimney or vent is actually pulling

What the measured tune-up catches before winter does

  • Combustion analysis — Flue gas sampling at steady fire and at ignition, logged in writing. This is where the invisible failures surface.
  • Heat exchanger inspection — Video scope inside the exchanger; look for cracks or deterioration that would spill gas into living space.
  • Airflow and temperature rise — Measure supply-to-return air temp differential against nameplate spec. Starved airflow predicts exchanger cracking years early.
  • Electrical diagnostics — Flame signal in microamps, ignitor resistance in ohms. These numbers predict lockouts weeks before they happen.
  • Condensate system (90+ furnaces) — Trap pulled, flushed, and drained. A plugged trap causes more midwinter shutdowns than combustion issues.
  • Flue and chimney check — Draft measurement, chimney liner condition assessment, orphaned water heater detection. In Altoona's coal-conversion homes, this is critical.

Altoona's railroad-era chimneys: what they need now

A large share of Altoona's housing went up for Pennsylvania Railroad families between the 1880s and the 1920s — the two-story frame houses that fill Juniata, Fairview, and Llyswen — and those homes carry masonry chimneys built for coal.

Here is the pattern we watch for: the furnace gets upgraded to a sidewall-vented condensing model, and the gas water heater is left behind as the only appliance on a chimney sized for two. Venting pros call it an orphaned water heater.

The oversized flue lets exhaust cool and condense inside the masonry, the liner deteriorates, draft weakens, and the risk of spilling combustion gases into the basement grows each season. A thorough visit checks the water heater's draft and the chimney's condition, not just the furnace's.

The other local fact is arithmetic: Blair County's heating season runs weeks longer on both ends than southeastern Pennsylvania's, so a furnace here logs far more firing hours per calendar year — and wear parts age on hours, not birthdays. That is also why October appointments are calm; November gets triage.

Tune-up questions Altoona homeowners ask

When should I book, and does timing really matter?

September through mid-October is the window. The furnace can be test-fired and corrected before you depend on it nightly, parts that measure marginal can be ordered without urgency, and you are ahead of the first-cold-snap rush that compresses everyone's schedule once the valley turns cold.

My furnace is only a few years old. Is this necessary?

Yes, for two reasons. Condensing furnaces have a drainage system that clogs regardless of age, and most manufacturer warranties expect documented annual maintenance. A young furnace also deserves a baseline: readings recorded now make drift obvious in year six instead of mysterious in year twelve.

I have CO alarms. Doesn't that cover the safety side?

Alarms are the last line, not the first. Consumer CO alarms are designed to tolerate low concentrations for hours before sounding, because they exist to prevent acute poisoning — not to flag a furnace drifting out of tune. An analyzer reads the problem at its source, at levels an alarm will never mention. Keep the alarms; add the measurement.

What does this cost, and what am I getting?

A full combustion tune-up visit takes roughly an hour and ends with your furnace's readings in writing — oxygen, CO, stack temperature, draft, flame signal, temperature rise, electrical readings — plus a baseline to compare against next fall. You are getting knowledge, not just a service. A furnace that measures healthy is worth paying to know about.
